# Can a Machine Be a Witness?

*Three senses of a word we have stopped distinguishing*

The question sounds as if it should have a clean answer. Machines now record,
measure, infer, and report. They are cited in courtrooms and quoted in
newspapers. They produce sentences that look exactly like the sentences by
which people tell us what happened. Either they can bear witness or they
cannot, and it seems the sort of thing a careful argument ought to settle.

A careful argument cannot settle it as one question, because it is not one
question. The word witness carries at least three distinct roles, and machines
stand in a different relation to each. The practical danger is not only that we
answer badly. It is that machine output quietly inherits an authority belonging
to a sense of the word it does not occupy.

## A Distinction Latin Kept And English Lost

Giorgio Agamben opens Remnants of Auschwitz with a philological distinction that
still does real conceptual work. In Latin, he notes, there are two words for
witness. Testis names a third party in a dispute. Superstes names one who lived
through an event from beginning to end.

These are not two shades of one idea. Testis is positional: external to the
contesting parties, useful to adjudication. Superstes is experiential: internal
to the event, marked by having undergone it. The first role can in principle be
filled by anyone suitably situated. The second cannot be filled by one who was
not there.

Agamben presses this distinction immediately in his reading of Primo Levi: Levi
is not a third-party factual observer for trial purposes but a survivor whose
authority is bound to what he underwent. That is why survivor testimony cannot
be reduced to a deposition without loss.

English collapsed these roles into one word. The collapse remains mostly
invisible until a new kind of speaker appears: one that can convincingly occupy
one role while failing another.

## First Sense: Testis (Third-Party Source)

In the testis sense, the answer is already yes. Machines can function as
witnesses, and the deeper question is how they should be tested.

Andrea Roth's Machine Testimony argues that courts have often treated machine
evidence incoherently, forcing it into categories built for other things.
Nevertheless, she shows that many machine outputs introduce dangers structurally
analogous to familiar testimonial dangers: opacity, analytical unsoundness,
poorly explainable transformations, and hidden design assumptions.

Her conclusion is strong and practical: some machine sources should be treated
as witnesses in confrontation contexts, with rights of challenge and meaningful
impeachment. In this sense, witness-status is not about consciousness. It is
about accountable doubt.

On that frame, machines qualify: they can and should be interrogated as sources
whose outputs require scrutiny.

## Second Sense: Assertion And Answerability

Between evidentiary source and lived-through witness lies assertion. Human
witnesses do not only emit information. They assert under norms that expose them
to being called to account.

Regina Rini captures this by noting that testimony carries implied commitments to
sincerity and competence. Recordings have historically stabilized these norms by
serving as an epistemic backstop: they can confirm, challenge, and discipline
public claims.

Can machines assert in this normative sense? Iwan Williams and Tim Bayne argue
that treating chatbot output as either full assertion or mere string emission is
too crude. They propose a middle category, proto-assertion.

Proto-assertion explains the current state well: machine outputs have many
functional features of assertion but lack full answerability. Systems can be
corrected, retrained, audited, or withdrawn. They cannot bear responsibility in
the way a person can.

In this second sense, the honest answer is neither yes nor no. It is not yet,
and possibly not ever, unless institutional forms of accountability are built
that do more than rename output as commitment.

## Third Sense: Superstes (One Who Lived Through)

Here the answer closes conceptually.

A superstes is not merely present; a superstes has undergone. A system can
record every second of an atrocity and still not undergo it. Better sensors,
more compute, or richer language generation do not by themselves bridge this
gap.

The crucial premise should be explicit: undergoing is not exhausted by causal
exposure. A functionalist may reject this premise, and that objection is serious
rather than superficial.

The asymmetry Agamben identifies strengthens the point. In extreme historical
contexts, those with maximal authority to witness can be those least able to
speak. Capacity to output testimony and authority to bear witness diverge.

Set against that asymmetry, machine systems sit in the inverse position:
maximally capable of producing testimony-shaped prose, with no standing in the
superstes sense.

## Explicit Objection: Is This Anthropocentric?

Strong objection: answerability criteria prove too much. We often rely on the
testimony of the dead, who cannot now be cross-examined or held accountable. If
present answerability were required, much of historical testimony would collapse.

Best available reply: relocate answerability temporally. What matters is not
that the speaker can answer now, but that at the time of assertion the speaker
stood within norms of accountability and was positioned as one who could be
contradicted. On this account, historical witnesses can remain witnesses while
machines still fail the criterion.

This reply is not cost-free. It risks looking ad hoc, because it is tuned to
preserve intuitions about difficult counterexamples. That vulnerability should be
admitted, not hidden.

## Explicit Unresolved Questions

Two questions remain open.

1. If proto-assertion is developmental, can institutional design create forms of
   machine answerability strong enough to count as assertion rather than
   simulation?
2. The functionalist challenge to superstes stands. If undergoing is ultimately
   reducible to suitable causal-functional histories, the third distinction may
   narrow. This article does not settle that dispute.

These open points do not dissolve the argument. They bound it.

## What Follows For Practice

Keep the three senses separated in institutions handling machine output.

- In the first sense, expand robust credibility-testing for machine sources.
- In the second sense, avoid pretending fluent output equals accountable
  assertion.
- In the third sense, refuse category drift that grants survivor-like moral
  authority to systems that do not undergo.

The key policy warning is not anti-technology. It is anti-conflation.
Machines can strengthen evidentiary practice when treated as testable sources.
They can distort it when treated as witnesses in senses they do not occupy.

## Method And Limits Note

This article follows an evidence-first discipline and source-scope declaration.
Claims are separated into source-backed claims, inferences, and authorial
assessments.

Source scope used:

- Agamben, Remnants of Auschwitz (testis/superstes distinction)
- Roth, Machine Testimony (legal-confrontation framing for machine evidence)
- Rini, Deepfakes and the Epistemic Backstop (testimonial norms and backstop)
- Williams and Bayne, Chatting with Bots (proto-assertion)

Unsupported-claim flags:

- The claim that machine output is culturally acquiring survivor-witness
  authority is treated as critical assessment, not as source-proven finding.
- The extension from Agamben's concept to machine systems is an inference and
  not attributable to Agamben as an explicit conclusion.

Coverage limits:

- Narrow source base for a broad question.
- No full treatment of wider epistemology-of-testimony literature.
- No full treatment of epistemic injustice literature, which is directly
  relevant to credibility allocation.
